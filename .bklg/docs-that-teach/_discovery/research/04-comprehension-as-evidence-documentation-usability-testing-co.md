# Comprehension as evidence — documentation usability testing, cognitive walkthrough and non-author reader studies; how a friction log is run and recorded so it is evidence rather than opinion

## Findings

- **The friction log is a named, specified artefact with a template, not a diary.** Google's internal practice (as documented publicly by a Developer Relations Manager who learned it there) fixes four things before logging starts: a one-to-two-sentence *scenario* stated in user terms ("Upload a picture of my cat to Google Cloud Storage"), a logger identity and context (platform/language/date/product), a chronological log of every action taken *including search terms typed and links followed*, and a stoplight severity mark — red/yellow/green — applied inline as the log is written, not retrofitted afterward. The scenario is called "the most crucial part": too narrow and it proves nothing generalizable, too esoteric and readers dismiss the finding as an edge case. [Hammerly, "An introduction to friction logging," developerrelations.com]
- **Being a non-author is treated as a methodological requirement, not a convenience.** The same source states outright that new hires make the best friction loggers *because* they lack the inside knowledge that lets an insider unconsciously route around a rough spot, and instructs loggers to "forget your inside knowledge and approach the problem as a user would." This is the mechanism, not just the intuition, behind the brief's insistence on a reader who is not the author. [Hammerly, developerrelations.com]
- **A friction log is evidence only once it is routed to someone who can act on it — logging without a destination is opinion by another name.** Google's process explicitly makes this the final, non-optional step: logs are submitted through a channel that routes them to the owning team, who correlate findings with existing bugs and respond in the log itself. A log that is written and filed away, with no one obligated to read it, does not clear the bar the source itself sets for the method. [Hammerly, developerrelations.com]
- **The friction log is explicitly distinguished from acceptance/scenario testing by intent, not procedure**: acceptance testing asks "did it work," friction logging asks "where was it rough," and records the rough spots even when the task technically succeeded. This is the same distinction the seed draws between "checked rather than asserted" compilation and a reader's actual experience — a page can compile and still be where a reader gets stuck. [Hammerly, developerrelations.com]
- **There is a published, non-Google taxonomy of three document-usability techniques, each keyed to what the document is *for*** — a direct match for the seed's "which need does this page answer" discipline. Ginny Redish (co-author of the foundational *A Practical Guide to Usability Testing*, 1999) and Caroline Jarrett give: **paraphrase testing** ("tell me in your own words," reader restates each unit of text in their own words — for documents meant to explain something in detail); **plus-minus testing** (reader marks the margin + or − as they read, then is interviewed on each mark — for documents meant to build general understanding or an emotional response such as trust); and **task-based/scenario testing** (reader is given a task and watched finding and using the answer — for documents whose job is to answer a question or give instructions). The table mapping technique to purpose is explicit and reusable as-is. [Jarrett & Redish, effortmark.co.uk; Redish, "Testing Paper Documents," digital.gov]
- **Cognitive walkthrough is a distinct, cheaper, non-empirical method that does not require an actual reader** — it substitutes a small group of evaluators (often including the writers) simulating a "typical" reader through a fixed four-question protocol at each step of a task: will they try the right action, will they notice it's available, will they associate it with the goal, will they see progress once they act. Originated by Lewis, Polson, Wharton & Rieman (1990) for "walk-up-and-use" interfaces and formalized as a practitioner's guide by Wharton et al. (1994); a "streamlined" two-question version (Spencer, 2000) exists for fast-paced teams. Because it uses a hypothetical user rather than a real one, the method's own literature is explicit that it is a *complement* to, not a substitute for, testing with actual non-author readers — a "no" answer at any step is a plausible defect, not a proven one. [Wharton et al. 1994, cited via textbookofusability.com; Wilson, "Cognitive Walkthrough," cs.uwaterloo.ca; Lewis, Polson, Wharton & Rieman 1990, CHI'90]
- **Small samples are legitimate evidence for a qualitative comprehension study, and the underlying math is published.** Nielsen and Landauer's model (1993) — the basis for the famous "test with 5 users" heuristic — shows a single qualitative session already surfaces roughly a third of a design's usability problems, and each usability problem a real reader hits is treated as *already proven* to need fixing (it does not need to recur across many readers to count, "if one person falls into a pothole, you know you need to fix it"). This directly supports treating one dated, non-author friction log as sufficient qualitative evidence — provided it is read as "these are real problems," not "this is the complete list of problems." [Nielsen & Landauer 1993; nngroup.com "Why You Only Need to Test with 5 Users"; nngroup.com "5 Participants Are Okay in a Qualitative Study"]
- **A published severity scale turns a friction log's stumbles into a rankable, comparable record rather than a mood report.** Nielsen's 0–4 severity scale (not a problem / cosmetic / minor / major / catastrophe) is designed to be applied by more than one rater and converges quickly — three raters' averaged scores are described as "satisfactory for many practical purposes." Combined with the friction log's own red/yellow/green stoplight convention, a friction log can carry both a narrative (what happened) and a severity signal (what to fix first) without inventing new instrumentation. [Nielsen, "Severity Ratings for Usability Problems," nngroup.com]
- **Concurrent think-aloud (talking while doing) surfaces different, and typically more, findings than a retrospective summary — but costs real task-time inflation, which is itself something to record rather than fight.** A 2024 meta-analysis (29 studies, 42 comparisons) found concurrent think-aloud lengthens task time (by roughly 17–20% in later controlled studies) but that its live verbalizations catch usability problems that pure behavioral observation misses; retrospective think-aloud (summarizing after the fact) trades some of that immediacy for realism and can under- or over-report depending on memory decay. For a documentation friction log this argues for capturing reactions *as they happen* ("now I'm frustrated," "copy-pasted without reading the prose") rather than a post-hoc summary, exactly as Google's template already does — and against treating elapsed time as a clean, comparable metric across sessions. [Hertzum, "Concurrent or Retrospective Thinking Aloud in Usability Tests? A Meta-Analytic Review," TOCHI 2024; Lewis & Sauro, measuringu.com]
- **Compiled-prose tooling in the Rust ecosystem (rustdoc doctests, `mdbook test`) proves examples still run against the current API; it proves nothing about whether a reader understood them.** Both tools re-execute the code inside a page as a test and fail the build if it no longer compiles or its assertions fail — that is real, gate-worthy evidence against API drift, but it is evidence of an entirely different claim than "a reader built the right mental model," and the tooling itself makes no claim otherwise. [Rust rustdoc book, "Documentation tests"; mdBook, "The test command"]
- **The current edition of the field's own practitioner reference names friction logs and non-author user research as a distinct, expected skill for documentation work**, alongside drafting, measuring and maintaining docs — i.e., this is treated in the profession as core practice, not a novel proposal. [Bhatti, Corleissen, Lambourne, Nunez, Waterhouse, *Docs for Developers*, 2nd ed. 2026]

## Implications for the idea

- **The brief's "friction log, dated" proof artefact has a load-bearing shape it does not yet have on the page, and this research supplies it without inventing anything new**: a named scenario stated the way a reader would state it (not "read the docs" but something like "model a course enrollment as a decision against this crate, from `cargo add` to a passing test"), a logger who states up front they are not the author and have not read the source, a chronological record of what was typed/searched/clicked, reactions captured *as they occur* rather than summarized afterward, and a severity mark per stumble. Adopting this shape turns "a friction log exists and is dated" into something a reviewer can actually audit for rigor, not just presence.
- **Non-authorship is not a nice-to-have for the logger — the evidence says it changes what gets found.** Whoever is recruited for `examples/outside-projection-adapter`-style comprehension work should be someone who has not read `happenstance-core`'s source and ideally has not read this repository's ADRs or evaluation corpus either; an author or a long-time contributor asked to "pretend" to be a newcomer is documented to unconsciously route around the exact rough spots the exercise exists to catch.
- **The three-technique taxonomy (paraphrase / plus-minus / task-based) maps cleanly onto the seed's own three named audiences**, which gives distillation a ready-made way to decide *which* comprehension check a given page needs rather than defaulting to one generic "have someone read it" pass: application authors modeling a decision are a task-based fit (can they find and use the answer — e.g., can they get from `cargo add` to a correct append condition); the evaluator reading for twenty minutes is a plus-minus fit (does the page build confidence, where does it lose it); a conceptual page like the DCB mental-model explanation is a paraphrase fit (can a reader restate, in their own words, what a dynamic consistency boundary is after reading the passage that is supposed to teach it). This gives "which need does this page answer" a testable consequence, not just a naming discipline.
- **Cognitive walkthrough is a legitimate, cheap first pass the authors themselves can run before spending a real non-author reader's time** — walking the four Wharton questions against a page's worked example, in the same room, before commissioning the friction log. It is explicitly not a substitute for the reader study; the literature's own framing is that a walkthrough's "no" is a hypothesis about a defect, and only a real reader's stumble makes it a fact. This gives the initiative a way to cheaply pre-screen pages (e.g., the `Tags::empty()` opening example the seed already flags) without treating that pre-screen as the proof artefact itself.
- **One dated friction log is defensible as *the* proof artefact, provided the initiative is explicit that it is qualitative and about real, not exhaustive, problems** — the Nielsen/Landauer math the seed's own vision implicitly rests on says a single non-author session will not find every comprehension failure, but every failure it does find is already real and does not need to be replicated to justify fixing. This matters for scoping the proof artefact honestly: the deliverable is not "prove the docs are fully comprehensible," it is "prove at least one real reader's real stumbles were captured and are traceable," which is a much smaller and more finishable claim.
- **Recording reactions concurrently, not retrospectively, is itself a methodological choice worth stating rather than leaving implicit** — the research shows concurrent and retrospective think-aloud surface measurably different findings, and Google's own friction-log template already defaults to concurrent narration. Distillation should decide, and record, which mode the non-author reader session uses, rather than let it default silently to "whatever the person felt like writing afterward."
- **The gate step and the friction log are proven, in the literature and not just in this repository's own argument, to be non-redundant evidence for different claims.** rustdoc/`mdbook test` tooling is a real, working instrument for "does this example still compile against the live crate," and nothing in that tooling's own documentation claims it says anything about whether a reader understood what the example was for. This confirms — from outside this repository's own reasoning — that the brief is right to insist on two separate proof artefacts rather than treating a green compiled-prose gate as sufficient on its own.
- **A severity mark, applied at the moment of logging rather than assigned after the fact, is cheap to adopt and gives future work a way to prioritize which stumble gets fixed first** without requiring a second study — this is directly actionable for however the friction log's recording format ends up specified, independent of which chapter/hosting questions are still open.
